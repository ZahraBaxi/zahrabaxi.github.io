colophon

a colophon is a document (typically found at the end of a book or publication) that describes the tools and technologies used in it's development. a colophon in web is just about the same thing.

why a colophon?

A colophon is a document that describes the tools and technologies used in the development of a publication. In the context of a website, it serves a similar purpose, providing transparency about the technical aspects of the site. My introduction to the colophon was through many physical bookbinding publications I've been involved in, one of which is archived in the Bancroft Library at UC Berkeley. I've been creating colophons for specific projects for a while, but lately I've been seeing more and more designers and folks from other fields include colophons for their portfolio or website as a whole, as a way to showcase their process and tools.

philosophy

This site started in high school, when I was just learning the basics of web design to build a place for my art portfolio. It's since expanded into what you see now, but it has always been an ongoing creative making and learning process. Every time I pick up a new skill or get interested in something new, I come back find a way to implement and improve this website. The purpose of this colophon is to document the tools, technologies, and design choices of the current version of the site. This whole site has been coded by me from scratch and it's been an exciting experience learning how to build a website from the ground up and coding for the public web. Because I've been learning continuously since 2019 and have revised this site over and over, I hope that a colophon will help others understand the process and decisions behind the site as well as maybe encourage you to build your own website!

design

Ideations and prototype designs are made in Figma ↗, then coded from scratch by hand. Previous versions of this site have been flashy or themed but this time I wanted the palette to be simple to priotize my portfolio work. I chose a warm cream background, near-black text, and use blue as an accent for links (which use an arrow pointed diagonally up to the right, to indicate an external link that will open in a new tab) and highlights.

development

This site is hand-coded in plain HTML, CSS, and vanilla JavaScript, using no frameworks. I use Visual Studio Code ↗ (I previously used Atom). While coding from scratch adds a bit of complexity with a huge learning curve, it also keeps things simple by not relying on external libraries and I get to have things exactly the way I want them to be. Pages share a header and footer through a small partials.js script, mostly so I only have to update the copyright year in one place. The site is version-controlled with Git, and files pushed via GitHub Desktop ↗. Served for free via GitHub Pages ↗, with the source living on a public repo since 2019 (and seeing many, many evolutions). The domain is registered through Namecheap ↗. Contact and bug report forms run on Back4App ↗, a cloud backend built on Parse. I use Google Search Console ↗ to make sure pages stay properly indexed.

make your own website

Want to build a site like this one? Anyone can, and it is free!

start here click to expand click to collapse you can do this!

I think learning to build your own website is powerful and incredibly cost effective. You can make your own website for free, and you do not need a degree, an expensive computer, or any prior experience. I was a TA for the Interactive Media Design series at UC Davis, which was an introduction to web development, and I loved seeing my students realize they could do this on their own. Everything listed below is free to get you started on the web. I do encourage you to dive deeper, however, to learn how to customize it to be your own and understand web safety and accessibility compliance.

I try to make things accessible to everyone, and that includes learning how to build a website. This guide is for anyone who wants a place online to show their work, who have no background with coding. If anything here is confusing, send me a message through the about page and I will make it clearer.

Let's say you are Frodo Baggins! You live in the Shire and run Baggins Map Co., where you sell hand-drawn maps and illustrated stories. Customers keep asking where they can see your work, so you decide to build a website. Scrolling below, you'll find the ways that will guide you step by step.

how to peek at web code

My website code is public on GitHub ↗. Feel free to look around and learn from it. You can also see the code of almost any website, right in your browser:

  • Mac: press Option + Command + U in Chrome and Safari, or Command + U in Firefox
  • Windows: press Ctrl + U
  • Any computer: right click the page and choose "view page source"

Looking is great for learning. Copying someone else's whole site or work is not, so use what you see as inspiration.

step by step click to expand click to collapse 9 small steps from nothing to something!

You will use three free tools. GitHub keeps your website online. Visual Studio Code is where you write it. GitHub Desktop sends it out into the world. There are lots of other tools you can use in the future, free and paid, that can help you maintain certain aspects of your website.

  1. make a GitHub account

    Your website needs a place to live online. Go to github.com/signup ↗ and make a free account.

    Your username becomes part of your website address, so pick one you are happy to share. As Frodo, you pick frodo-baggins.

  2. make a repository

    A repository (people say "repo") is a folder for your website that lives on GitHub. Think of it as the home of your business site. It is where all your maps, stories, and pages will live.

    Click the + button in the top right corner, then click "New repository."

    Name it yourusername.github.io. Frodo's repo name is frodo-baggins.github.io. Choose "Public," then click "Create repository."

    GitHub will offer to add a "README" file. You do not need one, so you can skip it.

  3. turn the repository into a website

    Open your repo and click "Settings." Then click "Pages" in the menu on the left. Under "Build and deployment," set "Source" to "Deploy from a branch." Then choose the branch called main and the folder called / (root). Click "Save."

    Can't find main in the list? That is normal when a repo is empty. Do steps 4 to 9 first, then come back and do this step. (Some older repos call it master instead of main. Either one works.)

    This turns your folder into a real website that customers can visit. Your website will be live a couple of minutes after you finish. Wait a bit, then refresh the page. Frodo's address will be https://frodo-baggins.github.io. Yours will use your own username.

  4. install GitHub Desktop and Visual Studio Code

    Now get two free apps. Visual Studio Code (people say "VS Code") is where you write your website. Think of it as a notebook for websites. GitHub Desktop sends your work to GitHub so customers can see it.

    • GitHub Desktop ↗
    • Visual Studio Code ↗

    Next, give GitHub Desktop permission to send your files to your repo. Without this, publishing will not work.

    1. Open GitHub Desktop and click "Sign in to GitHub.com."
    2. Your web browser opens. Log in to GitHub if it asks, then click "Authorize."
    3. Go back to GitHub Desktop. It may ask for your name and email. These get attached to your work. If you see a "keep my email private" choice, check it.

  5. add Live Server to VS Code

    Live Server shows you your website while you build it. That way you can check that your maps look right before anyone else sees them.

    In VS Code, click the Extensions icon in the left bar. It looks like four little squares. Type "Live Server" in the search box. Pick the one made by Ritwick Dey (his name is under the title) and click "Install."

  6. copy your repository to your computer

    Now copy your repo from GitHub to your computer. This is called "cloning." It lets you work on your site at home.

    First, decide where the folder will live. Pick a place you will remember. I keep mine right on my desktop.

    In VS Code, click the Source Control icon in the left bar. It looks like a little branch. Click "Clone Repository" and choose your yourusername.github.io repo. VS Code may ask you to sign in to GitHub first. When it asks where to save the folder, pick your desktop or another easy spot. Click "Open" when it asks.

  7. make your first files

    In VS Code, use the "new file" icon to make your files. You need an HTML file and a CSS file. A JavaScript file is optional. Use these names:

    file names to use
    filewhat it does
    index.htmlYour home page. It must have this exact name. A browser looks for index.html first, like the front door of a shop.
    styles.cssThe looks of your site: colors, fonts, and spacing.
    scripts.jsOptional. Makes things move or change. You can skip it until you want it.

    Name tips: use all lowercase letters, no spaces, and use hyphens instead. For example, shire-maps.html. Extra pages can be called anything, like about.html.

    Not sure what to type inside them? Read "code basics" below, or use Frodo's starter files for a head start.

  8. see your website while you work

    In VS Code, right click index.html and choose "Open with Live Server." Your website opens in your browser. Now change a word, save your file, and watch it change. To save, press Command + S on a Mac or Ctrl + S on Windows.

  9. publish your website

    GitHub Desktop is how you send your work from your computer to GitHub. Two words you will see: "commit" means saving a snapshot of your changes, and "push" means sending that snapshot to GitHub.

    First time only: show GitHub Desktop where your folder is.

    1. Open GitHub Desktop. In the menu at the top of your screen, click "File," then "Add Local Repository."
    2. Click "Choose," find the repo folder you saved in step 6, and click "Add Repository."

    Every time you want to publish:

    1. Open GitHub Desktop. Check that your repo name, like frodo-baggins.github.io, shows at the top left. Your changed files show up in a list on the left.
    2. Type a short note in the "Summary" box at the bottom left, like "first draft."
    3. Click the blue "Commit to main" button.
    4. Click "Push origin" at the top. The first time, it may say "Publish branch" instead. Click that.

    Wait a couple of minutes and refresh your website address. Your site is live. Customers can now find Baggins Map Co. Do this step again any time you want to update your site, like when you finish a new map.

    If GitHub Desktop says it cannot push or asks you to log in, you need to give it permission again. On a Mac, click "GitHub Desktop" in the top menu, then "Settings." On Windows, click "File," then "Options." Open "Accounts," sign out, sign back in, and click "Authorize" in your browser. Then try "Push origin" again.

code basics click to expand click to collapse what tags like p, h1, and a mean

A website is made of a few simple pieces. You do not need to learn them all at once. Here is what each one does.

  • HTML is the words and parts of your page: headings, paragraphs, links, and pictures. Think of it as the skeleton.
  • CSS is how it looks: colors, fonts, and spacing. Think of it as the clothes.
  • JavaScript is what it does: menus that open, buttons that react. It is optional. Think of it as the movement.

tags: what does <p> mean?

HTML is written with tags. A tag is a label inside angle brackets, like <p>. The p stands for paragraph.

Most tags come in pairs. The first one opens, and the second one closes. The closing tag has a slash in it. Whatever sits in between is the content.

<p>I draw maps by hand.</p>

Some tags need extra information. This is called an attribute. It goes inside the opening tag, as a name and a value in quotes.

<a href="work.html">see my work</a>

Here, a makes a link, href is the attribute, and work.html is where the link goes. A few tags, like <img>, have no closing tag because they have no content in the middle.

a whole page

Put the tags together and you get a tiny website. Save this as index.html and open it with Live Server to see it.

<!DOCTYPE html>
<html lang="en">
<head>
    <title>Baggins Map Co.</title>
    <link rel="stylesheet" href="styles.css">
</head>
<body>
    <h1>Baggins Map Co.</h1>
    <p>I draw maps by hand.</p>
    <a href="work.html">see my work</a>
</body>
</html>

Tags can go inside other tags. Notice the h1, p, and a all sit inside body. Indenting, which means pushing lines in with the Tab key, makes this easier to read. The computer does not need it, but you will.

css: how it looks

CSS goes in your styles.css file. It is written as rules. Each rule has three parts: who to style, what to change, and how to change it.

p {
    color: #1B1B1B;
    font-size: 1.1rem;
}
  • p is the selector. It picks which part of the page to style. This rule styles every paragraph.
  • color and font-size are properties. They are the things you can change.
  • #1B1B1B and 1.1rem are values. They say how to change it. Each line ends with a semicolon.

You can give a tag a name with class, like <div class="card">. In CSS, a dot picks that name: .card styles everything with that class.

To connect your CSS to a page, add this line inside the head: <link rel="stylesheet" href="styles.css">. The starter files already do this.

javascript: what it does

JavaScript goes in your scripts.js file. You can skip it at first. Here is a tiny example from the starter files. It finds the year in your footer and keeps it up to date, so you never have to change it by hand. querySelector finds one thing on the page, and #year means the thing with the id "year."

// this is a comment
const yearSpot = document.querySelector("#year");
yearSpot.textContent = new Date().getFullYear();

Two slashes start a comment. A comment is a note to yourself. To connect a script to a page, add <script src="scripts.js" defer></script> inside the head.

comments

Every language lets you leave notes that the browser ignores. In HTML, a comment starts with <!-- and ends with -->. Use comments to explain things to your future self, or to hide something for now.

download all (.zip) index.html work.html about.html styles.css scripts.js

A quick way to spot comments:

  • In HTML, a comment starts with <!-- and ends with -->
  • In CSS, a comment starts with /* and ends with */
  • In JavaScript, a comment starts with //

The starter files use a font called Metamorphous. You can use any font you like. Go to Google Fonts ↗, pick one, and copy the link it gives you. Paste it where my font link is in each HTML file, then change the font name in styles.css. The comments in the files show you exactly where.

web safety and accessibility click to expand click to collapse how to make your site safer and easier for everyone

Once your site is live, you can (and should) make it safer and easier for everyone to use.

There are also official rules and policies about websites. Which ones apply depends on where you live, where your visitors live, and what your site does. I am not a lawyer, so treat this as a starting point and use the links below to learn more on your own.

keep it safe

  • Anything in a public repo can be seen by anyone. Never put passwords, private addresses, or phone numbers in your files.
  • Turn on HTTPS. In your repo, go to "Settings," then "Pages," and check "Enforce HTTPS." This puts a padlock next to your address. If the box is grayed out, wait a little and try again.
  • Be careful with your email address. Bots collect emails from websites and send spam. A contact form or a link to your social media is safer. I use a contact form on my own site.
  • Only use pictures and words that are yours, or that you have permission to use. Ask before you show other people's faces, names, or work, and give credit.
  • If you collect anything from visitors, like a newsletter sign up or a form, you may need a privacy policy. Many places have privacy laws, like GDPR in Europe and CCPA in California. The simplest way to stay safe is to collect nothing.
  • Say how people can use your work. A license, like a Creative Commons license ↗, makes that clear. See mine on the disclaimers page.

keep it accessible

Accessible means people with different needs can use your site. That includes people who are blind, people who cannot use a mouse, and people who need larger text. The starter files already do some of this for you.

  • Add alt text to every image. It is a short description of the picture for people who cannot see it. If you are an artist, describe your art.
  • Make sure your text is easy to read against the background. Dark text on a light background, or the other way around, works well.
  • Use headings in order: one h1, then h2, then h3. Screen readers use them like a table of contents.
  • Make sure you can use your whole site with just the Tab key. Do not remove the outline that shows where you are.
  • Do not use color alone to give information. Add words or icons too.
  • Test your site. WAVE ↗ is free and shows what to fix. Chrome also has a free tool called Lighthouse. Right click your page, choose "Inspect," and look for the Lighthouse tab.

rules and policies to read about

  • WCAG ↗ (Web Content Accessibility Guidelines) is the worldwide standard for accessible websites. Most laws point to it.
  • ADA web guidance ↗ explains how the Americans with Disabilities Act applies to websites in the United States.
  • Section 508 ↗ covers accessibility for US government sites, and for many schools and organizations that work with them.
  • The A11y Project checklist ↗ is a plain language list you can check off one item at a time.
  • WebAIM ↗ teaches web accessibility in simple terms.
  • GitHub Pages and HTTPS ↗ shows how to turn on HTTPS.
  • California privacy law (CCPA) ↗ is a good place to start reading about privacy policies.
