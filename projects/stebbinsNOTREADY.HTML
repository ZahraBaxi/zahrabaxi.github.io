stebbins cold canyon

heat awareness campaign

  • role: graduate student researcher
  • collaborators: Prof. Emily Schlickman (LAED), Prof. Glenda Drew (Design), UC Davis Natural Reserve System
  • tools: adobe creative suite, cricut software, html/css, javascript
  • context: research / campaign / reserved lands & park spaces

overview

Stebbins Cold Canyon is a popular hiking reserve near Lake Berryessa, managed jointly by the BLM and the UC Davis Natural Reserve System. It is beautiful and accessible, which means it draws visitors who often arrive underprepared. In summer 2025 alone, the reserve had 6 emergency helicopter rescues due to heat exhaustion and dehydration. The trails are steep, shade is intermittent, and temperatures can climb past 100°F by midday with little warning.

I collaborated with Professor Emily Schlickman from the Landscape Architecture + Environmental Design department on a heat awareness campaign designed to intervene at the right moment: when a hiker can still turn back. The campaign includes sandwich board signs placed at decision points along the trail, orange ground stakes with stamped messages, and sticker assets. The core design question was: how do you communicate urgency to someone mid-hike, before conditions become dangerous?

This project was also presented as a case study at the California Trails & Greenways Conference in 2026 as part of a larger session on planet-centered design on trails, co-presented with Professors Glenda Drew and Emily Schlickman.

the problem

The existing trailhead signage at Stebbins is dense, graffitied, and visually overwhelming. There is a "Beat the Heat" panel buried in a large information board at the entrance, but it competes with trail maps, rules, distances, and QR codes. It does not interrupt. It does not prioritize. A hiker who is already underprepared will not stop to read it.

The deeper issue is a gap between knowing a place and preparing for it. Many visitors arrive through AllTrails, see a "moderate" rating, and do not internalize what 105°F at 1 PM actually means on an exposed ridge. By the time they feel it, they are already in danger.

design approach

The campaign uses neon orange, a color that reads as alarm and interrupts the dry tan and green of the canyon landscape. It is deliberately loud for a natural space. Signs are placed at decision points where the trail starts to climb and where turning back is still easy. The message is stripped to the minimum: what you need to know right now, in the body copy you would read while pausing on a hill.

Three sandwich board signs carry different messages depending on location. At the trailhead: Heat Kills. Start early. Carry extra water. Pace yourself. Further up at the climb: Low on water? Feeling hot? Head Home. The hierarchy is intentional: the largest text is the action, the supporting copy is the condition. The goal was a sign that works in one second for someone who is not stopping to read.

Ground stakes with stamped orange discs reading "DRINK WATER!" are placed along the path at lower intervals, a smaller and more casual reminder that still carries the campaign's visual language.

installed

The signs were fabricated and installed on the trail in summer 2025. The orange metal A-frame structures were built to be stable in wind and visible from a distance. Each sign panel is weatherproof and replaceable. Ground stakes were hammered in at points where hikers naturally pause or slow, creating a rhythm of reminders along both the lower and upper trail.

the data behind it

To ground the design in something concrete, I compiled incident data from public social media posts going back to 2017, then cross-referenced each event with historical weather data from the Open-Meteo archive. The interactive tool below lets you explore the full dataset: incident type, time of day the call was made, and daily high temperature on each rescue date. The pattern is hard to miss.

california trails & greenways conference

In 2026, the Stebbins project was presented as a case study at the California Trails & Greenways Conference, as part of a session called "Trails by Design: Designing for Shared Ecological Understanding," co-presented with Professors Glenda Drew and Emily Schlickman. The session framed this work alongside the Yolo Basin project as examples of planet-centered design on trails: design that treats the ecological system as the primary subject and shapes human activity around it.

The session also included a hands-on zine-making workshop where attendees created their own single-sheet 8-panel zines about a place they know, using ecological understanding as the content framework.

reflection

This project pushed me to think about design spatially and temporally. The signs exist in a specific place, at a specific time of year, competing with heat and exhaustion for someone's attention. That constraint forced a level of copy editing and hierarchy work I would not have done otherwise. Every word had to earn its place.

It also made clear how much the existing signage was failing not because people ignored it, but because it was designed for someone standing still in a parking lot, not someone halfway up a ridge in July. Place matters. Moment matters. This project was about designing for both.

The data component came out of wanting to ground the design in something real. The rescue log and weather correlation made a clear case for why the signs had to be bold and direct. It also became a resource for the reserve itself, a record they did not previously have in one place.
