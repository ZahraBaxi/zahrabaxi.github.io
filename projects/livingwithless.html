living with less

a personal field guide to zero-waste living

  • type: ongoing personal project
  • topics: fashion & repair, fermentation, transportation, plastic-free swaps, bulk bins, reusables, cooking from scratch, zero-waste extremism, food waste

This is an ongoing guide to zero-waste living — not the aspirational, perfectly-curated kind, but the actual kind, built around things I already do: fermenting vegetables, mending jeans, riding my bike, buying from the bulk bins. It's not about having an empty trash can. It's about asking what we actually need, and what happens to things when we're done with them.

chapter 01

fashion & repair

I've been mending my own clothes for years — darning socks, patching jeans at the knee, embroidering over worn spots to turn them into something intentional. It started as frugality and turned into something I genuinely love doing. There's something quietly radical about choosing to fix instead of replace.

The numbers behind fast fashion are hard to sit with. Every year, 101 million tons of textile waste ends up in landfills globally. We're wearing our clothes 36% fewer times than we did in 2000, and less than 1% of clothing is recycled into new clothing. The industry accounts for 2–8% of global greenhouse gas emissions and 9% of the microplastic pollution reaching our oceans.

Mending isn't a perfect solution to a systemic problem, but it's a real one. Keeping a garment in use longer — through patching, darning, or embroidering — extends its life and keeps it out of the waste stream. And honestly, a visibly mended piece of clothing tells a story that a brand new one doesn't.

  • Learn one mending technique at a time — sashiko, visible mending, or basic darning are great starting points
  • Buy secondhand first; thrift stores and clothing swaps beat anything new
  • Choose natural fibers (linen, wool, cotton) — they're more repairable and biodegradable
  • Avoid synthetic blends; they shed microplastics with every wash

chapter 02

fermentation & preservation

My kitchen counter usually has something going: a jar of pickles in brine, a sourdough starter that needs feeding, yogurt hanging in cheesecloth. Fermentation is one of the oldest food preservation methods humans have, and it turns out it's also one of the most practical tools for reducing waste at home.

When we cook, we tend to use only part of each ingredient — leaving peels, stems, and imperfect produce unused. We throw away roughly a quarter of what we buy. Fermentation gives those scraps a second life: cucumber ends become pickles, vegetable trimmings go into brine, overripe fruit becomes something new. It extends shelf life without any packaging, any refrigeration energy, and often actually increases the nutritional value of the food.

Making yogurt from scratch is simpler than it sounds: warm milk, add a spoonful of live yogurt, keep it warm for a few hours. No plastic tub, no foil lid, no drive to the store. A sourdough starter — just flour and water — ferments into something alive that can persist indefinitely. These aren't sacrifices — they're genuinely better versions of the things you already eat.

  • Start with lacto-fermented pickles — just vegetables, salt, water, and a jar
  • A sourdough starter lives indefinitely on flour and water; no commercial yeast needed
  • Yogurt just needs milk and a few tablespoons of live-culture yogurt as a starter
  • Ferment imperfect or surplus produce before it goes bad

chapter 03

getting around

Davis is one of the most bikeable cities in the country, which makes this one almost embarrassingly easy here. I bike everywhere — to campus, to the farmers market, to the grocery store. I also volunteer as a bike mechanic at the Bike Garage, which means I spend time helping people keep their existing bikes running instead of replacing them.

That repair piece matters as much as the riding piece. A bike that gets maintained and fixed when something breaks stays out of the waste stream. The most sustainable vehicle is the one that already exists and keeps working.

The environmental math on cycling is stark. Cyclists have 84% lower lifecycle carbon emissions than non-cyclists. Replacing even one car trip per day with cycling for 200 days a year cuts your mobility-related emissions by roughly half a ton annually.

  • Learn basic bike maintenance — fixing a flat, adjusting brakes, lubricating a chain — so small problems don't sideline you
  • Find your local bike co-op for tools, parts, and community
  • For longer or loaded trips, cargo bikes and e-bikes make car-free living more practical
  • Combine biking with transit for longer distances

chapter 04

plastic-free swaps

Going plastic-free isn't about purity. 91% of plastic waste isn't recycled — the recycling system as most of us know it mostly doesn't work. The only reliable option is to bring less plastic home in the first place.

Some swaps are obvious and easy: a reusable water bottle, a tote bag, a bar of shampoo instead of a plastic bottle. Others take a little habit-building: bringing containers to the deli counter, choosing glass jars over plastic tubs, skipping pre-packaged produce in favor of loose. Over 40% of all plastic waste comes from packaging — meaning the problem largely isn't with what we do after we have it, it's with how much comes in the door.

  • Bar soap, shampoo bars, and conditioner bars eliminate a whole category of plastic
  • Beeswax wraps replace cling film for covering food
  • Cloth produce bags instead of plastic ones at the grocery store
  • Concentrated cleaning tablets in a glass spray bottle instead of plastic jugs
  • Bring your own container to delis, bakeries, takeout spots — most places will fill them
  • Choose paper, glass, or metal packaging when plastic isn't avoidable

chapter 05

the bulk aisle

The bulk aisle is one of the simplest and most underused tools for reducing packaging waste. Grains, legumes, nuts, flours, spices, oats, coffee — things most of us buy in plastic bags every week — are available packaged in nothing, as long as you bring your own container.

It also changes how you shop. Buying bulk means buying exactly what you need — no more, no less. You're less likely to overbuy and let things go bad. Refilling the same jars over and over is genuinely satisfying in a way that tearing open a new plastic bag isn't.

Davis has good options: the Davis Food Co-op has an extensive bulk section, and the farmers market is a great source for local produce without packaging. Once you get into the habit of bringing jars, it becomes automatic.

  • Bring clean glass jars or cloth bags — weigh them empty first (taring) so you're not charged for the container
  • Stock up on staples: oats, lentils, rice, flour, nuts, dried fruit, spices
  • Buy only what you'll actually use — the goal is less waste, not more stockpiling
  • Support stores that maintain bulk sections; it signals demand

chapter 06

reusables out

Bringing your own container to a restaurant or coffee shop still feels weird to some people. It shouldn't. Disposable foodservice packaging is one of the largest single-use plastic categories — cups, lids, clamshells, bags — and most of it is not recyclable because it's contaminated with food. It goes straight to landfill.

In most places, all you have to do is ask. Cafes will usually fill a travel mug. A lot of restaurants will pack your leftovers in a container you bring. Some cities now have ordinances requiring them to accept customer containers. The barrier is mostly just the moment of asking.

In the US, 50 billion disposable coffee cups are thrown away each year. Coffee cups look like paper but are lined with polyethylene — that lining makes them non-recyclable and non-compostable in most facilities. A travel mug used daily for a year replaces 365 of them.

  • Keep a travel mug and a container in your bag — the habit only works if they're with you
  • Ask before assuming a place won't accommodate — most will
  • For leftovers: a wide-mouth glass jar or a lightweight stainless container works for almost anything
  • Some cities (like Berkeley, CA) have passed ordinances requiring foodservice businesses to accept clean customer containers

chapter 07

cooking from scratch

Most of the packaging I was throwing away used to come from food. Not from big-ticket items but from the small things — the yogurt tub, the bag of granola, the can of soup, the plastic-wrapped block of tofu. Processing and packaging food accounts for around 29% of all food system greenhouse gas emissions. The packaging often outlasts the food by decades.

Making things from scratch isn't the same as making everything from scratch. I'm not growing my own wheat. But yogurt from milk, granola from oats, hummus from dried chickpeas, stock from vegetable scraps — these take 20 minutes or less and replace a category of purchased-and-packaged products entirely. Dried pulses instead of canned ones cut packaging and are usually cheaper. Making your own bread eliminates the plastic bag.

The packaging math is significant but the food waste math might be bigger. Cooking from base ingredients means you use exactly what you need. Nothing comes pre-portioned for someone else's idea of a serving.

  • Dried beans: soak overnight, simmer for an hour — far cheaper and less packaging than canned
  • Vegetable scrap stock: save onion skins, carrot tops, celery ends in a freezer bag and simmer when full
  • Granola: oats, oil, maple syrup, nuts — 25 minutes, no bag
  • Homemade yogurt stores for 2 weeks and makes more than any tub you'd buy

chapter 08

the one-jar method

There's a corner of zero-waste culture built around keeping an entire year's trash in a single mason jar. If you've spent any time in these spaces online, you've seen it: the photo, the jar, the pristine kitchen. Lauren Singer, Bea Johnson — the people who made this famous are real and their practices are real. It's also a genuinely extreme approach that works for very few people.

My honest take: it's not a useful target. It treats a systemic packaging problem as a matter of individual perfection, and it sets up a standard most people will fail immediately and quit. A person who reduces their waste by 60% and keeps at it is doing more than someone who achieves the jar for six months and burns out.

What the jar method gets right is making waste visible. When you have to look at what you throw away, you make different decisions. That part is genuinely useful — not as a competition but as attention. I keep a small tray where my non-recyclable waste goes. I don't weigh it, I don't Instagram it, but I do look at it.

The things that make the biggest difference are not about willpower or aesthetics. They're about systems: what you buy, where you shop, how your home is set up. The jar is a metric. The habits are the point.

  • Make your waste visible without making it a performance — a tray or small bin for landfill-only items is enough
  • Focus on categories, not totals: which type of waste dominates? Start there
  • Perfection as a goal causes abandonment — consistency matters more
  • A 60% reduction maintained over years beats a 100% reduction abandoned after three months

chapter 09

food waste

If food waste were a country, it would be the third-largest emitter of greenhouse gases on the planet, after the US and China. Around a third of all food produced globally never gets eaten. A lot of that happens at the household level — things bought with intention and then forgotten in the back of the fridge.

Food waste in landfill is particularly bad because organic matter decomposes anaerobically there, producing methane — a greenhouse gas roughly 80 times more potent than CO₂ over 20 years. Keeping food out of landfill matters even after you've already wasted it.

At home, the most effective interventions are boring: meal planning, using up before buying more, understanding what "best by" dates actually mean (spoiler: almost nothing except quality), and learning to cook with whatever's about to turn. Fermentation helps here too — it's a preservation method that turns imminent food waste into something shelf-stable.

  • "Best by" dates indicate peak quality, not safety — most foods are fine well past them
  • First in, first out: put new groceries behind older ones
  • A weekly "use it up" meal from whatever needs to go prevents most fridge waste
  • Compost what you can't use — even backyard compost is significantly better than landfill
  • Freeze anything approaching its limit — bread, fruit, cooked grains, meat
